An xG experiment

The Premier League, replayed 10,000 times

2024/25 season · data from Understat About the data. Every shot in the 2024/25 Premier League season, with Understat's xG for it. The simulation re-rolls each shot as an independent Bernoulli(xG) trial and rebuilds the table from scratch. That's a simplification:

  • Shots are treated as independent — no game-state, momentum, or "game management" effects.
  • Own goals aren't modelled (Understat doesn't attribute an xG to them).
  • Ties are broken by points → goal difference → goals for, not head-to-head.

Every shot in football has some chance of going in — that's what xG (expected goals) measures. What actually happens over 90 minutes is one draw from that process. So what if we drew again?

A near-certain goal, missed

This chance had a 96% xG — the kind of shot that goes in almost every time it's taken. Marcus Tavernier put it wide.

Multiply that across 9,878 shots in a season and you start to see the problem with treating the table as a pure measure of quality: some of it is just how the coin landed.

A near-impossible goal, scored

And the opposite happens too. This one had less than a 1% xG — a shot that, on quality alone, should almost never go in. Alex Iwobi scored anyway.

Neither of these is a data error. They're just what "probability" means: even a 96% chance fails sometimes, and even a 1% chance comes off sometimes.

So we rolled the dice again

Take every one of the 9,878 shots from the real 2024/25 season and give each one its own weighted coin flip, using its actual xG as the probability of scoring. Rebuild the scorelines, rebuild the table.

Then do that 10,000 times, and see what changes and what doesn't.

Liverpool still win — usually

Across 10,000 replays, Liverpool are champions 75.7% of the time. That's a genuinely dominant number, but it also means that in roughly 1 in 4 replays of the exact same chances they actually created, somebody else wins the league.

Arsenal pick up most of the rest (17%). But look further down: Bournemouth — 9th in reality — out-title Chelsea, Newcastle and Aston Villa combined.

A deserved title, mostly

75.7% isn't "inevitable," but it's a lot better than a coin flip. Liverpool's actual title looks like the single most likely outcome of the season they played — not a lucky break, and not a guarantee either.

Who got the rub of the green?

Comparing each team's real finish to their average finish across 10,000 replays shows who the chances actually favoured. Nottingham Forest's real 7th-place finish sits well above what their underlying shot quality alone would predict — one of the most talked-about overperformances of the season.

Tottenham and Manchester United sit at the other extreme: mid-table by the shots they actually created, but well below that in reality.

When the minnows win it

In 31 of 10,000 seasons (0.31%), the simulated champion is a team that really finished in the bottom half of the table. Fulham (11th in reality) top the list, followed by Crystal Palace (12th).

Rare — but not impossible. That's the honest range of outcomes hiding inside one real season's worth of chances.

The games that got out of hand

Somewhere in the 10,000 replays, Manchester United and Tottenham somehow combined for 16 goals in one match. It's an outlier — but high-scoring thrillers (7+ goals) show up 21.1 times per replayed season on average, roughly double the real season's tally of 10.

When one player gets hot

In one replay, Erling Haaland scored 7 goals in a single match against Southampton. Individual hauls like this are rarer than team-level thrillers, but hat-tricks (3+ goals in a game) still land about 17–18 times per replayed season.

One honest caveat

The simulated seasons average 3.26 goals per game, noticeably more than the real season's 2.93. That's a known quirk of xG models: they tend to run a little "hot" relative to how teams actually finish, since finishing quality, last-ditch defending and goalkeeping aren't fully priced into a shot's raw quality.

Worth keeping in mind when reading the thriller and hat-trick numbers above — the shape of the story holds, but the exact rates are probably a touch inflated.
